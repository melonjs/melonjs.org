## Part 3-1: The art

A platformer needs two kinds of image, and they work differently.

A **tileset** is a grid of small square pictures: grass, dirt, a crate, a
mushroom. The level you draw in Part 3-2 is made of references to squares in
this grid, which is why a whole level file is tiny. Yours is
`src/data/img/tileset.png`.

A **sprite atlas** is one image holding every frame of a character's animation,
plus a `.json` file saying where each frame sits inside it. Yours is
`texture.png` with `texture.json` next to it. The character's walk cycle is the
frames named `walk0001.png` through `walk0011.png` inside that atlas, and Part
3-3 plays them back.

Both are already in your project from Part 2, so you can go straight to
[Part 3-2](/tutorial/part-3-2-modifying-the-level) and come back here when you
want the game to look like yours.

## Drawing your own

Open `src/data/img/tileset.png` in [Aseprite](https://www.aseprite.org). The
important habit, whatever tool you use: **draw on a new layer** rather than on
the original. You can hide it, erase it, or start over without having lost
anything. Copying a change onto every frame of an animation is also far easier
when the change is on its own layer.

{/* SCREENSHOT: a tileset open in Aseprite with a new layer added on top */}

Save, and the page in your browser reloads with your art in it. There is no
export step and no build to run: the development server from Part 2 is watching
the file.

If you draw a new character rather than editing the existing one, keep the frame
size the same as the frames in `texture.png`. Change the size and the atlas no
longer describes the image, so the animation in Part 3-3 will play the wrong
rectangles.

One melonJS convenience worth knowing when you go further: it reads Aseprite's
own `.aseprite` files directly, including the frame tags you use to name
animations, so a saved file can go straight into `src/data/img` with no PNG and
no atlas JSON to keep in sync.
